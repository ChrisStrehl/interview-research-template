# Onboarding and activation teardown

The one document in the workspace that a hiring manager will remember, because it is about the part
of the product every candidate could have used and almost none did. It is also the part of the
product the user can honestly have an opinion about after one session, which is not true of
retention or monetisation.

## Structure

1. **First value, defined.** What "activated" means for this product, in one sentence, from the
   walk. If the product itself defines it (a completed task, a first payout, a first project), use
   that.
2. **The path, step by step.** A numbered table: step, what the screen asks, what it gives, inputs
   required, decisions required, time, screenshot. Count the total steps and inputs before first
   value.
3. **What it assumes.** About the user's intent, device, patience and prior knowledge. Each
   assumption is a hypothesis the company has made; name them.
4. **Where a new user drops.** The three steps most likely to lose people, each with the reason
   ([O], argued from the walk and from review themes [R]), and the signal that would confirm it.
5. **What it measures.** From the analytics events observed during the walk. If an event fires on
   completion of onboarding, the company already tracks activation; say what it seems to be called.
6. **What is good.** Be specific and fair. A teardown that finds only faults is not credible.
7. **The first experiment.** One change, one metric, one reason, sized to a two-week test. This is
   the line the user can say out loud.

## Rules

- Opinion is expected here and marked [O]. Observation is [V]. Keep them on separate lines.
- Use the product's own words for screens and features.
- Compare to nothing. "Better than competitor X" belongs in the market front; this document is about
  this product and its new user.
- Under-promise. "I would test whether moving the login after the first task lifts completion" is
  stronger than "the onboarding is broken and I would fix it".
